MUN committee flow

Decided and built  Ben approved this flow and the recommendations on 27 Oct 2026, and added student tabs and friends and foes sheets. Decisions D-068 to D-072.

A committee session that runs like a real UN meeting: the debate happens out loud in the room, and the program keeps the record. It holds the speakers list, the motions, the amendment text, the timers and the votes, and shows them to everyone. Debate cycles between speeches, caucuses and amendment votes until the committee votes to close debate and vote on the resolution as a whole.

The floor: where debate happens

After the opening statements the Chair opens the floor. Each motion that passes sends the committee somewhere; when that ends, the floor reopens. This repeats for as long as debate needs, across lessons if necessary, until a motion to close debate passes.

Handling a motion

Order of precedence

When several motions are on the floor, the Chair takes them in this order. Caucus motions with longer times go first. This is the order already in the app’s rules of procedure (editable in Admin).

  1. Adjourn the meeting
  2. Suspend the meeting
  3. Close debate and move to voting
  4. Divide the question
  5. Unmoderated caucus
  6. Moderated caucus
  7. Set speaking time
  8. Introduce an amendment

Points (any time)

Personal privilege: a delegate can’t hear, or needs something. It is the only point that may interrupt a speaker.
Order: the procedure was not followed.
Parliamentary inquiry: a question to the Chair about the rules.
Right of reply: a delegation whose country was insulted asks to respond; the Chair decides.

The Chair logs a point with one tap if it matters for the record. Most points are simply answered aloud.

An amendment’s life

Every submitted amendment is visible to everyone, shown as tracked changes against the current text. Anyone can suggest an edit to someone else’s amendment; the sponsors accept or decline it, and each accepted edit is a new version. This replaces amendments to amendments, which get confusing in a classroom.

Decisions

Ben accepted every recommendation, with two changes, marked below.

  1. Who moderates: the Chair or the Secretary-General?Decided: call the moderator the Chair (or President), as the UN and most school conferences do, and keep Secretary-General for the person running the whole conference. The app can use either title.
  2. How much support does an amendment need to reach the floor?Decided: one fifth of the delegations that have students, minimum two. Computed seats can’t sign, so “one fifth of the committee” would be 7 of 33 GA seats: more than a class can reach (D-072).
  3. How is an amendment debated?Decided: two speakers for and two against by default, then a vote; delegates can move to vote sooner.
  4. Amendments to amendments?Decided: no. Use suggested edits, which the sponsors accept or decline.
  5. Closing debate: two-thirds or simple majority?Decided: two-thirds (the current setting), so a large bloc can’t cut debate short.
  6. Placards or devices for votes?Decided: placards for procedural votes, with the Chair entering the count; devices for amendment and resolution votes, so the record and the whip-count scoring are exact. The Chair can switch either way.
  7. Unmoderated caucus limit?Decided: 10 minutes per motion as you asked, extendable by a new motion; the Chair can set a total cap per lesson.
  8. Opening statement length?Decided: 60 seconds for Years 9–10 and 90 seconds for seniors, editable.
  9. If the resolution fails?Decided: the floor reopens with a second draft if one is ready; otherwise go to the debrief.
  10. Can students raise motions from their devices?Decided (Ben): yes, with tabs for motions, caucus votes and points. Students still say them aloud and the Chair decides (D-069).

Rules that stay as they are. The program never writes a student’s statement, clause or amendment. Country positions are labelled as sourced, estimate only or unknown. Whip counts are scored against the votes actually cast. Empty seats are computed and always labelled.
